At the edge of a wide valley, a narrow road followed the river toward a small town. The journey was quiet at first. Rain tapped the roof of the carriage, and the wheels made a steady sound over the stones. Mara watched the hills pass by and tried to remember the directions her brother had written on a scrap of paper. The ink had blurred in the rain, leaving only a few names legible.

When the road reached the old bridge, the driver slowed. A wooden sign leaned beside the gate, its painted letters almost hidden beneath moss. Mara stepped down and read it aloud, more to steady herself than to learn anything new. On the other side, a lantern glowed in the window of an abandoned tollhouse. She had expected the place to be empty, but a thin line of smoke rose from the chimney.

An elderly keeper opened the door before she could knock. He wore a wool coat with brass buttons and held a little notebook in one hand. He asked where she was going, then studied the paper she showed him. For a moment he said nothing. At last he pointed toward a path that climbed the wooded slope. It was not the route marked on her map, but he explained that the lower road had washed out during the storm.

Mara thanked him and started uphill. The path was steep, though the trees broke the wind and kept most of the rain away. She passed a stone wall, a stand of silver birches, and a field where several horses watched her from the far fence. Their coats were dark with water. One horse lifted its head as she passed, then returned to grazing.

Near the top of the hill, the clouds began to separate. A strip of pale evening light settled across the valley, brightening the river below. The town stood beyond it, gathered around a square tower with a clock that had stopped at seven. Mara checked her own watch. It was nearly half past five, so the clock had been wrong for some time.

At the crossroads she found a second sign, this one carved into a flat stone. The letters matched the name on her paper. She turned east and followed the ridge until she saw a farmhouse with a red door. Her brother had described it exactly: three windows facing the road, a blue pump near the steps, and a crooked apple tree beside the barn.

Before she reached the gate, someone came out to meet her. Her brother looked tired, but his smile was unmistakable. He took the wet map from her hand and laughed when he saw the ink. They went inside, where a kettle was already warming on the stove. Outside, the last rain moved east over the hills, and the river carried its silver reflection through the darkening valley.

After supper, her brother showed her the small workshop behind the house. He had spent the summer repairing clocks for the farms along the ridge, and several unfinished pieces rested on a long bench. Brass gears, wooden cases, and tiny screws had been arranged in careful rows. Mara noticed the clock from the town square among them. Its hands were loose, but the mechanism was clean. Her brother said he planned to return it once he understood why it had stopped.

They searched through his notes together. A page from early spring mentioned a cracked spring, while a later entry described a missing pin. Neither explained why the clock had remained at seven after the repair. Mara held a lamp while he opened the back panel. Inside, they found a folded scrap of paper tucked between the gears. It carried a date and a name neither of them recognized. They decided to ask the keeper at the tollhouse about it when they passed that way again.

They spent the evening comparing the journey with the stories they had heard as children. The tollhouse keeper, the broken bridge, and the stopped clock all seemed ordinary when described separately. Together they made the road feel like a small mystery that had been waiting for them. By morning, the clouds had cleared, and the valley looked entirely different in the sunlight. Mara folded the old map and placed it beside the window, ready for another trip.
